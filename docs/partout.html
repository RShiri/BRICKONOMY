What these numbers assume

A part-out total is a theoretical maximum — what a set is worth if every last brick eventually finds a buyer. For the long tail of common parts that means years, or never. So the yield here is the part-out value at 70% realisation, less 10% selling fees, which is what plausibly reaches your pocket. BrickEconomy quotes the maximum; this quotes the outcome.

Landed cost means delivered. A listing priced in dollars carries VAT and shipping into Israel, so a set that looks cheap abroad often is not — and the cheapest row here is chosen after that, not before.

Adjusted margin discounts the paper margin by how often the set actually sells. A fat edge on something that trades twice a year ties your money up for months; a thinner one you can turn over is usually the better trade.

Two honest caveats. Sets marked sell whole fetch more intact than broken up — the part-out is the worse move there. And parting out is work: sorting, listing and posting hundreds of lots. None of these numbers price your time.
